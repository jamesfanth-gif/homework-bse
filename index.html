Question 1: Low-Pressure Steam Heater Piping Arrangements (Gravity Return) [20 Marks]

1. Number and Types of Steam Pipe Systems

In a low-rise town house provided with low-pressure steam heater piping arrangements for space heating, two (2) common designs of low-pressure steam pipe systems based on gravity return are used:

  1. Single-pipe gravity return system
  2. Two-pipe (2-pipe) gravity return system (which can be arranged with a dry return or a wet return)

2. Explanation of Operating Principles

  • (a) Single-Pipe Gravity Return System: A steam heating system supplies heat to a space by connecting a steam boiler through piping to heat-emitting equipment (radiators) in the space to be heated. In a single-pipe gravity return system, a single pipe circuit feeds steam from the boiler to all of the radiators in the system, and the cooled condensate (water) exiting the radiators drains back through the same pipe by gravity to return to the bottom of the boiler below the water line. Air vents are installed on each radiator and on the main steam pipe to release trapped air.
  • (b) Two-Pipe (2-Pipe) Gravity Return System: A two-pipe system consists of a separate steam supply circuit and a separate condensate return circuit. All radiators are connected in parallel to the steam supply pipe and individually connected at their outlets to the return circuit, which carries condensate back to the boiler by gravity. There are two alternative return pipe arrangements:
    • Dry return: Condensate return pipework located above the boiler water level.
    • Wet return: Condensate return pipework located below the boiler water level (fitted with a check valve before entering the boiler).
  • Advantages & Disadvantages: The heat-carrying capacity (both sensible and latent heat) of steam is higher than that of water, making steam systems suitable for long-distance circulation; running costs are lower in large-scale production (e.g., district heating or combined heat and power). However, the disposal of condensate and the elimination of air from lengthy pipelines are the two major problems.

Question 2: Conservation of Energy & 4 Energy Recovery Systems [30 Marks]

Part A: Main Concepts of the Two Directions of Energy Conservation

The conservation of energy has two possible directions: (1) Energy Saving and (2) Energy Recovery:

  1. Energy Saving: Under this category, more efficient methods and equipment are used so as to limit the amounts of energy used in the first place. Examples include the use of electric motors, fans, and pumps of higher efficiency; chillers with higher coefficients of performance (COP); and building envelopes with better thermal insulation.
  2. Energy Recovery: In many building and industrial systems, waste heat is produced. Energy recovery systems are designed to capture this waste heat and put it to other useful applications (such as preheating outdoor supply air or producing hot water for space heating).

Part B: Elaboration of All Four (4) Energy Recovery Devices

1. Heat Pipe Heat Exchangers (Ref: Lecture 1, Slides 27–30 / Lecture 2, Slides 18–24)

  • Appearance & Construction: Composed of multiple finned tubes (heat pipes) operating independently across a casing divided by a sealed central partition (preventing cross-contamination). Secondary finned surfaces (continuous plate fins, integral/embedded spiral, or welded spiral fins) increase heat transfer efficiency. Internally, each bare heat pipe is a permanently sealed vacuum tube with an internal capillary wick structure filled with a working heat transfer fluid, with no moving parts.
  • Working Principle: Operates on a closed-loop evaporation/condensation cycle in a counterflow arrangement. Hot exhaust air passes over the evaporation section, causing the internal working liquid to vaporize. Higher vapour pressure drives the vapour to the cooler condensation section, where cold supply air absorbs the released latent heat of vaporization (warming the supply air) and condenses the vapour back into liquid. The liquid returns to the hot end via the capillary wick (assisted by gravity when tilted at 5.7° or 10% slope).
  • Merits & Demerits: No moving parts, long life, high reliability, no external power required, no cross-contamination, and compact; however, it transfers only sensible heat (no latent heat recovery).
Fig. 1: 3-D Heat Pipe Heat Exchanger  |  Fig. 2: Bare Heat Pipe Operating Principle

2. Enthalpy Wheels (Heat Regenerators / Heat Wheels) (Ref: Lecture 1, Slides 32–34 / Lecture 2, Slides 26–31)

  • Appearance & Construction: Consists of a slow-rotating porous wheel with a magnified open honeycomb pattern (which constricts air to axial laminar flow) spanning across adjacent supply and exhaust air ducts, complete with a drive motor/belt mechanism, partitions, frames, air seals, and a purge section.
  • Working Principle: The hygroscopic wheel media absorbs and stores both sensible heat and moisture (latent heat) (recovering 70–90% of total energy). In Winter Operation, the wheel picks up heat and moisture from warm humidified room exhaust air and rotates into the cold outside air stream to supply warmed and humidified air to the room. In Summer Operation, hot moist outside air is cooled and dehumidified by transferring heat and moisture to the cooler, drier exhaust air stream. A purge section blows contaminants off the wheel to minimize cross-contamination.
  • Merits & Demerits: High total energy efficiency (sensible + latent); disadvantages include bulky size (2×2 m to 5×5 m), complicated ductwork, slight cross-contamination, corrosion risk, and regular maintenance required.

3. Heat Reclaim Condenser (Double-Bundle Condenser Chiller) (Ref: Lecture 1, Slides 36–37 / Lecture 2, Slides 33–35)

  • Appearance & Construction: A water chiller equipped with a double-bundle condenser containing two separate water tube bundles—a heating bundle (heat-recovery condenser) and a tower bundle (standard cooling condenser)—either enclosed in a single shell (Fig. 10) or mounted as twin shells alongside the evaporator and compressor (Fig. 8 & Fig. 9).
  • Working Principle: Provides simultaneous cooling and heating. The evaporator chills water for the building's cooling load (interior zone AHUs). High-temperature hot refrigerant gas from the compressor enters the double-bundle condenser: the heating bundle captures waste condensing heat to supply hot water via the hot water pump to perimeter fan-coil units (supplemented by an auxiliary boiler/heater if required), while any excess heat is rejected to the cooling tower via the tower bundle and condenser water pump.
Fig. 8, Fig. 9 & Fig. 10: Chiller with Heat Reclaim Condenser (System Schematic & Double-Bundle Shell)

4. Heat Pumps (Ref: Lecture 1, Slides 38–42 / Lecture 2, Slides 36–44)

  • Appearance & Principle of Operation: A vapour-compression unit (comprising an Evaporator, Compressor, Condenser, Expansion Valve, and a 4-Way Reversing Valve in reversible units) that absorbs low-grade heat $Q_2$ from a heat source at lower temperature $T_2$ using mechanical work input $W$, and delivers upgraded heat $Q_1$ at higher temperature $T_1$.
    COPhp = Q1 / W = Q1 / (Q1 − Q2) ≈ 3 (delivers 3 J of heat per 1 J of electrical work input)
  • Three Main Types in Notes: (a) Single-purpose heat pump (gas-to-gas, liquid-to-gas, or liquid-to-liquid); (b) Reversible heat pump (uses a 4-way valve to provide space cooling in summer and space heating in winter); and (c) Cascade chiller-heat pump system (chiller condenser acts as heat source for the heat pump evaporator for simultaneous cooling and heating at 48–82°C).

Question 3: Dalton's Law, Moist Air Properties & Psychrometric Chart [20 Marks]

1. Dalton's Law (Ref: Lecture 3, Slide 4)

Dalton's Law states that the total pressure of a gas mixture is equal to the sum of the partial pressures of the elements in the mixture which occupies a given volume at a given temperature:

Pm = P1 + P2 + P3 + ... + Pn

Applying Dalton's Law to moist air (a mixture of dry air and water vapour):

Pat = Pa + Pw

where Pat = atmospheric pressure of moist air (Pa), Pa = partial pressure of dry air (Pa), and Pw = partial pressure of water vapour (Pa).

Question 4: Cooling Coil Bypass Factor & Five (5) Psychrometric Processes [30 Marks]

Part A: Operating Principle of "Bypass Factor" of a Cooling Coil (Ref: Lecture 3, Slides 17–18)

  • Definition & Operating Principle: Bypass Factor (BF) is a function of the physical and operating characteristics of the cooling coil and represents the portion of the air which is considered to pass through the cooling coil without change of its operating condition.
  • Conditions Affecting Bypass Factor:
    1. Heat Transfer Surface Area: Decreasing the heat transfer surface of the cooling coil (fewer rows of coil, fewer fins per cm, wider tube spacing) results in an increase in Bypass Factor.
    2. Air Velocity: Decreasing the air velocity through the cooling coil results in a decrease in Bypass Factor (provides more residence time for air to contact the cooling coil fins).
  • Formula:   Bypass Factor = (to − ts) / (tm − ts) = (ho − hs) / (hm − hs) = (wo − ws) / (wm − ws)
    where subscript o = off-coil air, s = apparatus dew point (ADP), and m = air mixing point (on-coil air).

Part B: The Five (5) Psychrometric Processes & Flow Line Diagrams [6 Marks Each]

  1. (1) Cooling vs. Dehumidification (Fig. 1-4): Sensible cooling cools air at constant moisture content (when coil temp > dew point Td). In Cooling + Dehumidification (1 → 2), moist air passes over a cooling coil at a surface temperature below its dew point (Td), simultaneously reducing dry-bulb temperature (T1 → T2), moisture content (w1 → w2), and enthalpy (h1 → h2).
  2. (2) Heating vs. Humidification (Fig. 1-5): Heating (1 → 2) increases dry-bulb temperature (T1 → T2) and enthalpy (h1 → h2) at constant moisture content while reducing degree of saturation. Humidification (2 → 3) adds water spray or steam to increase moisture content toward saturation point s; spray chamber effectiveness is E = (h3 − h2) / (hs − h2).
  3. (3) Cooling + Dehumidification + Reheat (Fig. 1-6): Combination of cooling + dehumidification across a cooling coil (1 → 2, lowering temperature to T2 and removing moisture) followed by sensible heating across a reheat coil (2 → 3 at constant moisture content, raising temperature to T3 and enthalpy from h2 to h3).
  4. (4) Evaporative Cooling (Fig. 1-7): Also called the "Ideal Adiabatic Saturation Process". Sensible heat is removed and moisture is added simultaneously (1 → 2) as air passes through recirculated spray water with no external heat added or removed. The process follows the constant wet-bulb temperature line (T1 → T2, w1 → w2).
  5. (5) Air Mixing Process (Fig. 1-8): Return air (state R at Ti) and outdoor air (state O at To) are adiabatically mixed in the AHU mixing chamber to form mixed state M (Tm) along the straight line connecting them: % of re-circulation air = x / y and % of outdoor air = (y − x) / y.
