Question 1: Low-Pressure Steam Piping Arrangements based on Gravity Return 20 Marks

1. Steam Heating System & Gravity Return

A steam heating system supplies heat to a space by connecting a source of steam through piping to heat-emitting equipment (radiators) in the space. The steam gives up its heat in the radiators and condenses into water (condensate).

In a gravity return system, steam rises by buoyancy and condensate drains back to the boiler by gravity alone without any mechanical pump.

2. System Classification

There are two (2) common designs of low-pressure steam systems based on gravity return:

  1. Single-pipe gravity return system
  2. 2-pipe gravity return system

For the 2-pipe system, there are two possible return pipe arrangements:

  • (a) Dry return: Condensate pipework is installed above the boiler water level.
  • (b) Wet return: Condensate pipework is installed below the boiler water level.

3. Operating Principles

  • Single-pipe gravity return system: A single pipe serves both steam supply and condensate return. Steam travels along the top of the pitched pipe while condensate flows back along the bottom in the opposite direction. Air vents must be installed at radiators and the end of the steam main to discharge trapped air.
  • 2-pipe gravity return system: Steam supply and condensate return are separated into two distinct mains. Radiators are connected in parallel; each unit receives steam via a supply riser and discharges condensate through a return drop. In a wet return, a check valve is provided near the boiler to prevent boiler water backflow.

4. Advantages & Disadvantages of Steam Systems

  • Advantages: Steam has a higher heat carrying capacity (sensible and latent) than water, making it suitable for long-distance transport; lower running cost due to large-scale generation.
  • Disadvantages: Condensate disposal and air elimination from lengthy pipelines are the two major problems.

Question 2: Energy Saving vs. Energy Recovery & 3-D Devices 30 Marks

1. Concepts of Energy Conservation

  • Energy Saving: Using more efficient equipment and methods to limit the amounts of energy used. Examples: high-efficiency motors, fans, and pumps; chillers of higher COP; building envelopes with better thermal insulation.
  • Energy Recovery: Capturing waste heat produced by systems and putting it to other uses. Examples: heat pipe heat exchangers, enthalpy wheels, run-around coils, heat reclaim condensers, and heat pumps.

2. Device 1: Heat Pipe Heat Exchanger

  • Appearance & Construction (3-D): A rectangular casing housing an array of individual, permanently sealed finned tubes. A sealed partition plate separates the hot exhaust air stream and cold supply air stream in a counterflow arrangement, completely preventing cross-contamination.
  • Working Principle: Operates on a closed-loop evaporation/condensation cycle. Heat from the hot exhaust vaporizes the liquid inside the pipe; higher vapour pressure drives vapour to the cooler end, where it condenses and releases latent heat to the cold supply air. Condensate returns via an internal capillary wick and gravity. No moving parts and no external power required.
  • Factors Affecting Capacity: Wick design, tube diameter (15–50 mm), working fluid properties, temperature difference, and inclination tilt angle.
  • Disadvantage: Transfers sensible heat only; cannot recover latent heat.

3. Device 2: Enthalpy Wheel (Heat Wheel)

  • Appearance & Construction (3-D): Consists of a slowly rotating porous wheel fabricated from heat-and-moisture absorbing materials (honeycomb structure), a drive motor, casing, air seals, and counterflow supply/exhaust ductwork.
  • Working Principle: The wheel rotates slowly between the exhaust air stream and outdoor supply air stream. In winter, sensible heat and moisture from the warm exhaust air are picked up and stored by the wheel matrix, then transferred to the cold dry incoming supply air as the wheel rotates through it. Regeneration is continuous.
  • Efficiency & Merits: Recovers 70% to 90% of exhaust energy, transferring both sensible and latent heat.
  • Disadvantages: Bulky cross-section (2×2 m to 5×5 m); complex ductwork arrangement; requires routine maintenance; slight risk of cross-contamination (minimized by purge units).

Question 3: Dalton's Law, Moist Air Properties & Psychrometric Chart 20 Marks

1. Dalton's Law

Dalton's law states that the total pressure of a gas mixture is equal to the sum of the partial pressures of the elements in the mixture which occupies a given volume at a given temperature:

P_m = P_1 + P_2 + P_3 + ... + P_n

Applying Dalton's law to moist air (mixture of dry air and water vapour):

P_at = P_a + P_w

where:
$P_{at}$ = atmospheric pressure of moist air (Pa)
$P_a$ = partial pressure of dry air (Pa)
$P_w$ = partial pressure of water vapour (Pa)

2. Eleven (11) Properties of Moist Air

  1. Humidity ratio (w): Ratio between the mass of water vapour $m_w$ and mass of dry air $m_a$:
    w = m_w / m_a = 0.62198 · P_w / (P_at - P_w)   [kg/kg da]
  2. Dry bulb temperature (t): Temperature of moist air measured by a standard thermometer (°C).
  3. Wet bulb temperature (t*): Temperature measured by a thermometer wrapped in a wetted wick exposed to an air current of 1–2 m/s (°C).
  4. Dew point temperature (t_d): Temperature at which condensation of moisture begins at constant pressure and humidity ratio (°C).
  5. Moisture content: Weight of water vapour in kg per kg of dry air (kg/kg da).
  6. Relative humidity (φ): Ratio of actual water vapour partial pressure to saturated water vapour pressure at the same temperature (%).
  7. Degree of saturation (μ): Ratio of humidity ratio $w$ to saturated humidity ratio $w_s$ at the same temperature and pressure ($\mu = w/w_s$).
  8. Specific volume (v): Volume of moist air in $\text{m}^3$ per kg of dry air ($\text{m}^3/\text{kg da}$).
  9. Enthalpy (h): Amount of heat in moist air above an arbitrary datum (-17.8°C for dry air, 0°C water for vapour):
    h = 1.005 t + w(2501 + 1.87 t)   [kJ/kg]
  10. Sensible Heat Ratio (SHR): Ratio of sensible heat to total heat:
    SHR = Sensible Heat / (Sensible Heat + Latent Heat)
  11. Room Sensible Heat Ratio (RSHR):
    RSHR = Room Sensible Heat / (Room Sensible Heat + Room Latent Heat)

3. Six (6) Thermodynamic Properties on Psychrometric Chart

  1. Dry-bulb temperature (θ): Vertical lines (bottom horizontal axis, °C).
  2. Wet-bulb temperature (θ'): Diagonal lines sloping downward to the right (°C).
  3. Moisture content (g): Horizontal lines (right vertical axis, kg/kg da).
  4. Specific enthalpy (h): Diagonal lines sloping upward to the left (kJ/kg).
  5. Specific volume (v): Steeply inclined lines leaning to the left ($\text{m}^3/\text{kg}$).
  6. Relative humidity (H): Curved lines curving upward from the saturation curve (%).

Question 4: Bypass Factor & Five Air Conditioning Processes 30 Marks

1. Bypass Factor of Cooling Coil

  • Operating Principle: Bypass factor is a function of physical and operating characteristics of the cooling coil and represents the portion of air which is considered to pass through the cooling coil without change of its operating condition.
  • Affecting Conditions:
    • Decrease of heat transfer surface (fewer coil rows, fewer fins/cm, wider tube spacing) → Increases Bypass Factor.
    • Decrease of air velocity (providing more contact residence time) → Decreases Bypass Factor.
Bypass Factor = (t_o - t_s) / (t_m - t_s) = (h_o - h_s) / (h_m - h_s) = (w_o - w_s) / (w_m - w_s)

where: $t_m, h_m, w_m$ = entering mixing air; $t_o, h_o, w_o$ = off-coil condition; $t_s, h_s, w_s$ = apparatus dew point (ADP).

2. The Five (5) Air Conditioning Processes

(1) Cooling vs. Dehumidification (Cooling + Dehumidification)
Sensible cooling alone is represented by a horizontal line moving left ($w$ constant), whereas pure dehumidification is a vertical downward line ($t$ constant). In practical air conditioning, air passes over a cooling coil whose surface temperature is below the entering dew point ($T_d$); hence sensible cooling and dehumidification occur simultaneously. Dry bulb temperature ($t$), moisture content ($w$), and enthalpy ($h$) are all reduced.
Line on Chart: Straight line sloping downwards and to the left ($1 \to 2$) towards the saturation curve.

(2) Heating vs. Humidification (Heating + Humidification)
Sensible heating alone is a horizontal line moving right ($w$ constant, $t$ and $h$ increase). Humidification adds moisture to the air. In a combined process ($1 \to 2 \to 3$), air is first heated sensibly from $T_1$ to $T_2$ ($1 \to 2$), then humidified via steam or water spray ($2 \to 3$). Spray chamber effectiveness: $E = (h_3 - h_2) / (h_s - h_2)$.
Line on Chart: $1 \to 2$ is horizontal to the right; $2 \to 3$ slopes upwards and to the left towards saturation.

(3) Cooling + Dehumidification + Reheat
Air is first cooled and dehumidified below its dew point ($1 \to 2$) to extract excess moisture, and then sensibly reheated ($2 \to 3$) to a comfortable supply air temperature without changing moisture content. Prevents room overcooling while maintaining comfort humidity.
Line on Chart: $1 \to 2$ slopes downwards and to the left; $2 \to 3$ is strictly horizontal to the right ($w$ constant).

(4) Evaporative Cooling
Simultaneous removal of sensible heat and addition of moisture. The recirculated spray water remains at constant temperature along the wet-bulb temperature line without external heat addition or extraction (also called "Ideal Adiabatic Saturation Process").
Line on Chart: Moves upwards and to the left along the constant wet-bulb / enthalpy line ($1 \to 2$) towards the saturation curve ($t$ decreases, $w$ increases).

(5) Air Mixing Process
Return air ($1$) and outdoor fresh air ($o$) mix adiabatically in the mixing chamber of the AHU. The resulting mixing point ($m$) always lies on the straight line connecting $1$ and $o$.
% of re-circulation air = x / y   |   % of outdoor air = (y - x) / y
Line on Chart: A straight line joining point $1$ and point $o$, with mixing point $m$ located on the segment.
